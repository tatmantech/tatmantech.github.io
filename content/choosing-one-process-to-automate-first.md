Every post on this blog so far has picked one specific process and automated just that
one. This post is the framework behind *how* to pick it — because "what should we
automate first" is a harder question than it looks, and getting it wrong wastes the
scarcest resource a small business has: someone's uninterrupted afternoon.

<!-- more -->

## Three questions, in order

**1. Does it already happen the same way, repeatedly?** Automation rewards repetition. A
process you do differently every time isn't ready to automate — it's still being figured
out, and automating it early just locks in bad habits. A process you already do the same
way, over and over, without much thought, is exactly the kind of thing worth taking out of
your hands.

**2. Does it generate information you're currently throwing away?** This is the
highest-leverage signal, and it's the one most people miss. A process that produces a
byproduct — a resolved support ticket, a completed inspection, a filled-in log — is worth
automating even if the process itself is fine, because the automation payoff isn't just
speed, it's turning that discarded byproduct into a searchable asset. The ticket-tracking
post on this blog is exactly this: the tickets were already getting resolved fine; what
was missing was capturing the resolution as reusable knowledge.

**3. Is the cost of being wrong low?** Pick a first automation where a mistake is
annoying, not catastrophic. Not payroll. Not anything customer-facing without a review
step. This isn't about avoiding automation for high-stakes processes forever — it's about
building your own trust and understanding of how these tools behave before you point them
at something that matters more.

## Applying it: the ticketing example, worked

Run the framework against the maitake ticketing setup from the first post on this blog:

- **Repeatable?** Yes — support tickets come in constantly, in a consistent rough shape
  (a problem, a resolution).
- **Generating discarded information?** Yes, and this was the actual insight — every
  resolved ticket was a small piece of institutional knowledge disappearing into a closed
  email thread.
- **Low cost if wrong?** Yes — worst case, a ticket is mis-filed or the knowledge-base
  automation misses a pattern. Nobody's paycheck or customer relationship is at risk while
  you get the process right.

Three yeses. That's what made it the right *first* thing, not because ticketing is
inherently more important than other candidates, but because it scored well on all three
axes at once.

## What this framework is actually for

Not to find the *best possible* automation — to find the one you can start on Monday with
confidence it won't blow up in your face, and that teaches you something useful about
where the next one should be. Run any candidate process through these three questions
before committing an afternoon to it. If it doesn't clear all three, it's probably not
your first one — but it might be your second, once the first has taught you what actually
matters when picking.
